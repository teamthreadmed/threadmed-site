Obesity: What Harm, and Why

BMI is a reasonable first screen. The question that matters comes next: is this body fat causing harm, what harm, and why?

AdiposeCardiometabolicDiabetesPopulation health
Anthony Pick, MD, CDCES, CCD, MSCP

Consider two people, each with a BMI of 33. The first carries her weight at the hips and thighs. Her blood pressure, glucose, liver tests and sleep are normal, and she walks four miles a day.

The second has a 44-inch waist, treated hypertension, a fatty liver with early scarring and sleep apnea he has never had tested. (Both are illustrations, not real patients.)

BMI says both have obesity, and BMI is right about both. I have written before about why body size is the wrong target (Stop Chasing the Scale), so I will not reargue it here. The short version, which new data support, is that BMI is a mostly reasonable first screen.

What BMI cannot answer is the next question, and it is the one that matters. Is this body fat causing harm? What harm? And why?

In January 2025 a Lancet Commission built a diagnosis around that question. It defined preclinical obesity as excess body fat with organ function intact, and clinical obesity as excess fat that has begun to impair an organ or daily life, judged against 18 specific criteria. A study now published in Nature has tested the idea in 844,108 adults in China, the UK, the US and Spain. This essay is about what that study adds.

What the study adds

BMI mostly holds. Adding waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio left the classification unchanged in about 9 of 10 people. BMI over-called obesity in 1.2–3.1% and missed it in 5.2–8.6%. The errors sat at the margins and they mattered there: people in the overweight BMI range with confirmed central adiposity carried higher mortality, and people with a high BMI and a normal waist did not. The waist is a useful second check on BMI, not a replacement for it.

Most obesity is preclinical. Clinical obesity made up 22.7–31.3% of all obesity, or 5.6–10.9% of the whole population, depending on the cohort.

Harm, not size, separates the risk. Compared with people without obesity or organ dysfunction, clinical obesity carried a 69% higher risk of death (hazard ratio 1.69, 95% CI 1.64–1.73). Preclinical obesity carried a 19% higher risk (1.19, 1.14–1.24).

The fine print

The headline most people will remember is that 77–91% of those with preclinical obesity did not progress to clinical obesity over 10 years. That number is real, but it rests on two things a clinician should know.

First, an attribution discount. Not every new case of hypertension or atrial fibrillation in a person with obesity is caused by the obesity. The authors handled this sensibly: they multiplied the observed rate of new organ dysfunction by an attributable fraction of 0.38 to 0.52. Undo that step and the picture changes. By my back-calculation from their published figures, new organ dysfunction of any cause appeared in roughly 21% of the UK cohort and about half of the Chinese and Spanish cohorts over the decade.

The discount is defensible for counting disease in a population. It cannot tell me whether the hypertension in the patient in front of me is from adiposity. The authors say so directly.

Second, how hard anyone looked. Organ dysfunction was identified mostly from diagnostic codes in health records, not from standardized examination. The Chinese cohort, which examined participants every two years, found more of it. A practice that checks blood pressure properly, measures urine albumin and asks about sleep will find more still.

There is also a result that sits quietly in a sensitivity table. People with organ dysfunction and no obesity had a hazard ratio near 1.4. Dysfunction carries most of the mortality signal, with or without excess fat.

So I read “stable” with care. Preclinical obesity is not a verdict of safety. It is a state that stays preclinical only as long as someone keeps checking. The authors make a related point: the label describes current health and should not decide who is eligible for treatment, any more than prediabetes rules out care.

What harm, and why

The tool I use for this is a plain table. Each row reads left to right: where the fat is, how it could injure a tissue, the harm that results, and the measurement that would show it.

The discipline is in the middle columns. “At risk” is only useful when I can name the harm and say why it should occur in this person. Tongue and neck fat narrows an airway, so the harm is sleep apnea and the test is a sleep study. Fat delivered to the liver scars it, so the harm is fibrosis and the test is a fibrosis score.

Read that way, the Commission’s 18 criteria sort onto two axes.

The metabolic axis is glucose, triglycerides and HDL cholesterol, blood pressure, the liver and the kidney. These travel together. The older name for the cluster is metabolic syndrome; its liver expression is MASLD and MASH, and its heart and kidney expression is now called cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome. This axis follows where fat is stored more than how much there is. Visceral fat and fat deposited in the liver and muscle matter more than the number on the scale.

The organ and mechanical axis is sleep apnea, breathlessness, heart failure and atrial fibrillation, knee and hip arthritis, urinary incontinence, lymphedema, reproductive dysfunction and the ability to get through an ordinary day. Here the drivers are load and local fat: weight on a joint, tissue around an airway, pressure on the pelvic floor.

The two axes move independently. Blood glucose and sleep apnea often improve well before BMI reaches any target. Weight can fall while muscle and strength fall with it. A person can be far along one axis and nowhere on the other.

The Commission’s rules contain one quirk worth knowing. The metabolic criterion is met only by the full cluster of high glucose, high triglycerides and low HDL together. Type 2 diabetes alone does not make obesity “clinical,” while raised blood pressure alone does. The Commission debated this, and the criteria may be refined. It is a reminder that the label is a convention, and the physiology underneath is continuous.

What this changes in clinic

  1. Keep BMI and add the waist. A tape measure, the same site each time, alongside BMI. It is the cheapest test in this essay and the one the study most clearly supports.
  2. Look for dysfunction directly. Blood pressure taken properly. HbA1c and a lipid panel. Kidney function with a urine albumin. Liver enzymes with a fibrosis score. A few questions about snoring and daytime sleepiness. Grip strength and a timed chair stand.
  3. Repeat it. A single label expires. The value is in the trend on each axis, checked every 6 to 12 months.
  4. Treat toward function. Success is a blood pressure that needs less medication, a liver that is no longer scarring, sleep that restores, knees that allow a walk. Weight is one input to those outcomes.
  5. Do not use the label as a gate. Someone with preclinical obesity and type 2 diabetes still has diabetes that deserves treatment.

Body-composition scans and MRI have a place when the answer would change a decision. None of the four cohorts used them, so this study neither supports nor argues against routine imaging.

Trajectories, not boxes

Return to the two people with a BMI of 33. The useful question was never whether they “have obesity.” It is where each of them sits on each axis today, and which way they are moving.

BMI remains a reasonable first screen, and this study does not overturn it. What the Commission framework adds is the second question, and the study shows the answer tracks outcomes across four very different health systems. Its categories are still boxes drawn on continuous biology. The work in clinic is to name the harm, understand why it is there, measure it more than once, and act on the direction of travel.
