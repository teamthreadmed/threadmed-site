The Other Half of the Androgen Story

Testosterone in women: the evidence, the measurement problem, and why the logistics are the hardest part.

Gonadal axisBoneSexual functionMenopauseRegulation
Anthony Pick, MD, CDCES, CCD, MSCP

In men, we have a threshold that overreaches — a number treated as a diagnosis when it is really a population statistic.

In women, we have the opposite problem. There is no threshold at all. No validated biochemical definition of female androgen deficiency exists, the assays are worse at female concentrations than at male ones, and the one guideline-endorsed indication is diagnosed entirely on clinical grounds. And yet testosterone prescribing to women is growing fast, largely outside the evidence, and often in a form that cannot be undone.

Same thread, opposite failure. Both fail the individual for the same reason: the number and the person are not the same object.

Why this is complicated — the short version

  1. There is no such thing as a diagnostic testosterone level in women. The Global Consensus Position Statement is explicit: a blood total testosterone should not be used to diagnose the condition we treat.
  2. The assays are unreliable where it matters. Female concentrations sit near or below the floor of most immunoassays. LC-MS/MS is required, and even then reference ranges are inconsistent.
  3. Free testosterone is on shakier ground here than in men. The consensus panel recommended focusing on total testosterone as the biomarker, noting that the evidence that free testosterone is the biologically active fraction in women is lacking.
  4. Menopause is not an androgen cliff. Testosterone declines gradually from the twenties onward. Natural menopause produces no abrupt fall. Bilateral oophorectomy does.
  5. Oral estrogen creates the appearance of deficiency. It raises SHBG substantially, driving free testosterone down — a mechanism worth understanding before you attribute anything to the ovaries.
  6. Only one indication is evidence-based, and the effect is moderate: hypoactive sexual desire disorder in postmenopausal women, on average about one additional satisfying sexual event per month over placebo.
  7. The claims that sell — energy, mood, cognition, muscle, bone — are the ones without adequate data. Not disproven. Unproven, which is different, and not a basis for prescribing.
  8. There is no FDA-approved testosterone product for women in the United States. Australia has one; the UK licensed it in 2025; the US has never approved one and withdrew consideration in 2004–2005.
  9. That regulatory vacuum, not the physiology, drives most of the harm — male formulations dosed by eyeball, compounded creams of variable potency, and pellets that deliver supraphysiologic levels which cannot be retrieved.
  10. Long-term safety is genuinely unknown. Breast and cardiovascular outcome data at physiologic doses do not exist. Everyone prescribing this, including me, is working inside that gap.

Where I come to this from

Two credentials shaped how I read this literature, and both arrived through bone.

I certified in clinical densitometry (CCD) because the skeleton is where sex-steroid physiology becomes undeniable — you can argue about libido, but a hip fracture is not a matter of interpretation. Reading DXAs in midlife women for years made it obvious that the hormone conversation was being conducted badly, and that the cost was structural and permanent. That led me to the Menopause Society certification (MSCP), which I completed this year, largely because I wanted the whole framework rather than the endocrine slice of it.

Comprehensive care of women in midlife is not a subspecialty interest bolted onto endocrinology. It is one of the clearest examples of what happens when a system is managed one organ at a time — bone by one clinic, vasomotor symptoms by another, sexual function by nobody, and the hormone that connects them treated as a controversy rather than a physiology.

The shadow over the whole conversation

You cannot discuss testosterone in women without first acknowledging why estrogen is difficult to discuss, because the same history shapes both.

In 2002 the Women’s Health Initiative halted its estrogen-plus-progestin arm — oral conjugated equine estrogen with medroxyprogesterone acetate — reporting increased breast cancer and cardiovascular events. Prescribing collapsed within months, and a generation of clinicians completed training without learning to manage menopause at all.

What was less well communicated, then and since:

  • The trial population averaged 63 years of age, with only a minority within ten years of menopause. It was designed as a chronic disease prevention study in older women, not a symptom-management study in newly menopausal ones — and the results were generalized to a population it never enrolled.
  • The estrogen-alone arm told a different story. In women with prior hysterectomy, conjugated estrogen significantly reduced breast cancer incidence, a finding that has held up in randomized trial meta-analysis and that most clinicians still don’t know.
  • Long-term follow-up through nearly two decades showed no increase in all-cause mortality in either arm.
  • Subsequent trials designed around the timing hypothesis — ELITE, KEEPS, DOPS — supported a more favorable vascular profile when therapy starts near the menopausal transition. Current society guidance accordingly supports hormone therapy for symptomatic women under 60 or within ten years of menopause.

Avrum Bluming and Carol Tavris made the accessible version of this argument in Estrogen Matters, and it has been influential — deservedly, in that it reached clinicians and patients that journal correspondence never would.

And here is where I want to be careful, because this is exactly the kind of history that gets flattened in the retelling. WHI was not wrong. Its findings remain valid for the regimen and the population it studied: the estrogen-plus-progestin result — increased breast cancer incidence persisting through twenty years of follow-up — is real and reproducible. What failed was the extrapolation, in both directions. Applying a result from oral CEE+MPA in 63-year-olds to every estrogen, every route, and every age was an error. Concluding now that hormone therapy is broadly protective and that the original trial was simply a mistake is the same error, running the other way. The WHI investigators themselves have pushed back publicly on recent characterizations of their data, and they are right to.

The relevance to testosterone is direct. That history produced two things at once: a genuine, decades-long undertreatment of menopausal women, and a commercial vacuum that has since been filled by people selling certainty. Both of those are still operating, and both shape how testosterone is now being marketed to women.

The physiology, briefly

Women produce more testosterone by mass than estradiol. Roughly half comes from the ovaries and half from peripheral conversion of adrenal precursors — DHEA and DHEAS, and androstenedione — with the balance shifting across the lifespan. Testosterone is also the obligate substrate for estradiol: aromatase in ovary, adipose, bone, and brain makes the estrogen from the androgen.

Two facts do most of the clinical work:

The decline is gradual, not menopausal. Circulating androgens fall progressively from the third decade. By the time a woman reaches natural menopause her testosterone is already well below its twenty-something peak, and it does not drop off a cliff at the final menstrual period the way estradiol does. This is why “my testosterone crashed at menopause” is usually not what happened, and why menopausal status is a poor guide to who is androgen-insufficient.

Surgical menopause is different. Bilateral oophorectomy removes roughly half the androgen supply at a stroke. That is a genuine, abrupt androgen deficiency state, and it is the population in which the clinical case is strongest — even though, frustratingly, it is not the population most of the trials studied.

Adrenal insufficiency, hypopituitarism, and chronic glucocorticoid exposure also produce real androgen deficiency in women. These are diagnoses of the axis, not of the symptom, and they are worth thinking of before anything else.

Testosterone is a female hormone. Estradiol is a male hormone.

I wrote in the companion essay that estradiol is a male hormone — that it is the dominant sex steroid for the male skeleton, that Mendelian randomization puts estradiol and not testosterone on the causal path to fracture in men, and that suppressing it with an aromatase inhibitor to make a testosterone number look better is a bad trade the patient is rarely told about.

The mirror statement is equally true and equally resisted: testosterone is a female hormone. Women produce more of it by mass than they produce estradiol. It is present throughout the female lifespan, it acts on receptors in muscle, bone, brain, and genital tissue, and it is the obligate substrate from which estradiol is made. A woman with no testosterone has no estrogen.

The sex-labelling of these hormones is a linguistic accident with real clinical consequences, and it produces symmetrical errors:

  • In men, it licenses the crushing of estradiol as though it were contamination — and the skeleton pays.
  • In women, it makes testosterone sound like a foreign substance requiring extraordinary justification, so the conversation never happens — and then, in reaction, it becomes an exotic enhancement sold at a premium.

Neither error survives contact with the physiology. There are no male and female hormones; there are shared hormones in different proportions, acting on the same receptors, in bodies with different amounts of aromatase, SHBG, and receptor density. Everything downstream of that — the assays, the reference ranges, the guidelines, the marketing — is built on a taxonomy the endocrinology doesn’t support.

This is the systems point, and it is why I keep returning to it: we did not decide these were sex-specific hormones because the biology told us so. We decided it first and have been correcting for it ever since.

What can actually be diagnosed

Here is the part that surprises people, including clinicians who prescribe this regularly.

There is no validated syndrome of female androgen deficiency. Attempts to define one biochemically have failed, because symptom prevalence does not track testosterone concentration in women across the physiologic range. Studies looking for a level below which women reliably develop low desire, low energy, or low wellbeing have not found one.

So the diagnosis is clinical. The condition with an evidence base is hypoactive sexual desire disorder — low sexual desire, present for at least six months, causing personal distress, and not better explained by something else. (DSM-5 folded HSDD into female sexual interest/arousal disorder; the trials and guidelines continue to use HSDD because it is more cleanly operationalized.)

The distress criterion is not a formality. A woman with low desire who is not troubled by it does not have a disorder, and treating her converts a preference into a pathology.

Before attributing anything to androgens, the differential deserves genuine work:

  • Relationship factors — the single most common driver, and the one a prescription cannot touch
  • Depression, anxiety, and the medications that treat them — SSRIs are a leading iatrogenic cause
  • Genitourinary syndrome of menopause — dyspareunia suppresses desire reliably; local estrogen or intravaginal DHEA (prasterone) often solves what looked like an androgen problem
  • Sleep, including undiagnosed sleep apnea in midlife women, which is systematically missed
  • Thyroid disease, iron deficiency, hyperprolactinemia
  • Vasomotor symptoms and the exhaustion they cause — treat the menopause first and reassess
  • Alcohol, chronic illness, life load

If desire improves when the estrogen, the sleep, the SSRI, or the relationship is addressed, that was the diagnosis.

Why the lab is nearly useless here — and what it is actually for

Testosterone assays were designed around male concentrations. Women circulate roughly 15–70 ng/dL depending on age and assay; immunoassays perform poorly in that range and become unreliable at the lower end, which is precisely where the interesting values sit. LC-MS/MS is the only defensible method, and even then reference intervals vary between laboratories.

Calculated free testosterone inherits every one of those problems and adds SHBG. In women, SHBG is moved dramatically by oral estrogen — including combined oral contraceptives, which can raise it several-fold and suppress free testosterone accordingly. That is a real and reversible mechanism for low desire in a premenopausal woman, and switching from an oral to a transdermal route is a legitimate intervention. But it also means a calculated free testosterone in a woman on oral estrogen tells you about her binding globulin, not her ovaries.

So the honest position, which I think is underappreciated even among prescribers:

In women, measure testosterone for safety, not for diagnosis or efficacy. The baseline level exists to exclude a pathologically high value before you add more. The follow-up level exists to confirm you have not pushed her out of the physiologic range. It is a guardrail. It is not a target, and it is not a diagnostic test.

Every major statement in this field agrees, and yet the number is routinely used exactly as a target — which is how women end up on doses producing levels no premenopausal woman ever had.

The evidence: what testosterone does and doesn’t do

What it does. Meta-analysis of randomized trials in postmenopausal women shows that testosterone at physiologic doses improves sexual function: an average of roughly one additional satisfying sexual event per month over placebo, with improvements in desire, arousal, orgasm, pleasure, and responsiveness, and a reduction in sexual distress. This is Level I, Grade A evidence — the strongest in this entire field, male or female.

It is also, deliberately stated, a moderate effect. One event a month matters to many women and not to others, and that is a legitimate thing for a patient to weigh.

What it doesn’t do — or rather, what we can’t say it does. The consensus panel found insufficient data to support testosterone for any other symptom or condition in women, or for disease prevention. That list includes the entire marketing platform of the wellness industry: energy, fatigue, mood, cognition and “brain fog,” lean mass, athletic performance, bone density, and cardiovascular protection.

The distinction between unproven and disproven is worth holding honestly. Some of these may turn out to be real; androgen receptors exist throughout female muscle, bone, and brain, and the trials were never designed to answer these questions. But “we haven’t studied it” is not a reason to prescribe, and it is certainly not a reason to charge for it.

Risks at physiologic doses. Reassuring as far as it goes: meta-analyses show no severe adverse events with physiologic transdermal use. Acne and increased hair growth occur, generally mild and dose-related. Oral testosterone adversely affects lipids — raising LDL and lowering HDL — while transdermal does not, which is the main reason oral androgens (including methyltestosterone combinations) should not be used.

Risks we cannot quantify. Long-term safety has not been established. There are no adequate data on breast cancer or cardiovascular outcomes with physiologic testosterone in women, and the absence of those data is exactly why the FDA declined approval in 2004–2005. Women with hormone-sensitive malignancy sit outside the evidence entirely. Anyone who tells a patient this is “proven safe” is describing an absence of evidence as evidence of absence.

The logistics: where this actually breaks down

This is the part that separates people who prescribe testosterone to women from people who write about it.

There is no FDA-approved product. Australia’s TGA registered AndroFeme 1 — a 1% testosterone cream delivering female-range doses — in November 2020, and the UK’s MHRA licensed it in August 2025. Europe approved Intrinsa, a patch, in 2006 and it was withdrawn in 2012 for commercial reasons. The United States has never approved a testosterone product for women. A deuterated testosterone engineered to resist aromatization received FDA development-pathway guidance in early 2026 and is years from any approval decision.

So every American prescription is one of three imperfect things:

1. A male product, dose-reduced. Typically about a tenth of a male gel dose. It works, and the pharmacokinetics are the best-characterized of the three. The problems are practical and real: metered pumps and single-dose packets are not designed to be divided accurately, so day-to-day dosing is approximate; and transference matters more here than anywhere else in medicine. Testosterone gel transfers by skin contact, and the people most likely to be in contact with a midlife woman — her partner, her children, her grandchildren — are the people in whom exogenous androgen does the most harm. Application site, timing, covering, and hand-washing are not fussy details; they are the safety plan.

2. Compounded cream. More convenient dosing, and the formulation women most often prefer. But compounded preparations have no FDA oversight of potency or bioavailability, and professional societies caution against routine use for exactly that reason. Two grams of “the same” cream from two pharmacies are not the same drug.

3. Pellets and injections — which I will state plainly should not be used in women.

The Global Consensus Position Statement excludes injectables, pellets, and any preparation producing supraphysiologic concentrations from its recommendations. That exclusion is not fussiness, and the reasoning is the same principle that runs through the male essay: peak exposure is what causes harm, and irreversibility is what makes it unforgivable.

Consider the arithmetic. A premenopausal woman runs a total testosterone somewhere around 15–70 ng/dL. The smallest practically drawn dose of testosterone cypionate — a product formulated for men who need ten to twenty times a woman’s daily production — produces peaks multiples above that range within days. There is no female-scaled injectable. Splitting a male vial does not solve a pharmacokinetic problem; it just makes the overdose smaller.

Then there is the half-life. With a transdermal preparation, a woman who develops acne, deepening voice, or hair growth stops the cream and the exposure ends in a day. With an injection she waits weeks. With a pellet she waits months, and it cannot be retrieved. Some androgenic effects — voice change, clitoromegaly, male-pattern scalp hair loss — do not reverse when the exposure ends. A delivery system you cannot stop is the wrong delivery system for a drug whose main risk is dose-dependent and partly permanent.

Pellets compound this by design: supraphysiologic levels, no titration, no retrieval, no approved product, no long-term safety data — marketed most aggressively for the indications with the least evidence. It is the worst available arrangement, and it is the fastest growing.

And none of it is covered. No FDA indication means no insurance, which means cash pay, which means the patients who get treated are selected by income rather than by need — and which creates the commercial pressure that produced the pellet industry in the first place.

A practical protocol

For a postmenopausal woman with HSDD after the differential has been genuinely worked:

Before starting - Confirm the diagnosis clinically: low desire, ≥6 months, personal distress, alternatives addressed - Ensure adequate estrogen status and treat GSM first — dyspareunia will defeat any androgen - Baseline total testosterone by LC-MS/MS, to exclude an already-high value. Not to make the diagnosis - Baseline lipids; assess breast cancer history and risk - Informed consent that is actually informed: off-label, no approved product, moderate benefit, unknown long-term safety, and a defined trial period

Dosing - Transdermal only. Approximately one-tenth of a male dose, aiming to keep total testosterone within the premenopausal physiologic range - Never oral. Never pellets. Never injections - Explicit transference instructions, with the household in mind

Monitoring - Total testosterone at roughly 6 weeks and periodically thereafter — as a ceiling check, not a target to chase - Clinical review for acne and hirsutism at each visit; these are the early signals of overdose - Lipids periodically

Stopping - Reassess at 3–6 months using the same measure of desire and distress you started with - If there is no meaningful benefit, stop. The same discipline I argue for in men applies here with more force, because here the long-term safety data are thinner - If it works, continue with periodic reassessment of whether it still does

The market, fairly

The same critique I would make of men’s “optimization” clinics applies here, but it needs a different preface, because the history is different.

Women’s symptoms in midlife have been dismissed for decades. Menopause research was set back a generation by the misreading of a single trial. The FDA has approved no testosterone product for women in more than twenty years while approving many for men. Clinicians frequently have neither the training nor the visit length to work up low desire properly. The vacuum was not created by the companies now filling it, and women who felt unheard for years and finally found someone who took them seriously are not foolish.

But the response to a research vacuum should not be an irreversible implant. Pellets in women combine supraphysiologic dosing, no retrievability, no approved product, and no long-term safety data — and they are marketed for precisely the indications with the least evidence. When a clinic advertises testosterone for energy, focus, and body composition in women, it is selling the list the consensus panel specifically declined to endorse.

The honest position is uncomfortable and worth holding anyway: the evidence supports a moderate benefit for one clearly defined problem, using a formulation the United States does not approve, with long-term safety we do not know. Both the reflexive prescribers and the reflexive refusers are avoiding that sentence, in opposite directions.

What would change my mind

I would revise substantially if: an FDA-approved female-specific product reached market with real pharmacokinetic standardization; adequately powered trials reported breast and cardiovascular outcomes at physiologic doses over years rather than months; or trials designed to test energy, cognition, or musculoskeletal endpoints — rather than measuring them as afterthoughts in sexual-function studies — showed benefit.

Those are answerable questions. That they remain unanswered in 2026, for half the population, is its own finding.

The thread

In men, we have a number that pretends to be a diagnosis. In women, we have a diagnosis with no number and no approved drug to treat it. The male error is overconfidence in a threshold; the female error is a regulatory and evidentiary vacuum that the market filled with pellets.

Both are failures of the same kind. Androgen physiology runs through desire, bone, muscle, mood, metabolism, and the vasculature in both sexes, and in both sexes we have tried to manage that network by reading — or inventing — a single value.

The work is the same in either case: define the syndrome clinically, use the laboratory for what it can actually tell you, treat what is reversible first, prescribe within the evidence, and decide in advance how you will know whether it worked.
