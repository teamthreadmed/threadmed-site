The Trouble With Normal

Why a lab range is not the same as normal biology — and what TSH teaches us about asking “normal compared with whom?”

ThyroidLab medicineMenopausePopulation health
Anthony Pick, MD, CDCES, CCD, MSCP

A 58-year-old woman brings me two lab reports, nine years apart. In 2017 her TSH was 0.9 mIU/L. This spring it was 3.6. Both reports say the same thing in the column on the right: normal.

She is more tired than she used to be, colder, and her LDL has risen about 30 mg/dL. She is also two years past her final period, which on its own can account for much of that LDL change and some of the fatigue. She wants to know whether her thyroid is part of the story. The lab report has already answered — but it answered a different question from the one she asked.

Every lab report presents a number beside a reference range as though nature had drawn the line. It didn’t. We did. How we draw that line, and what it can and cannot tell us, is the subject of this essay — the first in a series on laboratory medicine. (The patient is a composite; the reasoning is not.)

The seductive simplicity of the bell curve

Most of us learned normality through the Gaussian distribution. In a true bell curve, about 68% of observations fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95.4% within two, and 99.7% within three. That gives us an intuitive model: the middle is common, common is normal, and the tails are suspect.

For many measurements this is a useful approximation. It has two problems.

First, many biological measurements are not normally distributed. Second, and more important, statistical normality and biological normality are different things. That has three consequences:

  • A value can be unusual without being disease.
  • A value can be common and still carry risk. An LDL of 130 mg/dL is unremarkable among American adults; it is not what human arteries are built for.
  • A value that is normal for the population may not be normal for you.

TSH illustrates all three unusually well.

TSH doesn’t read the statistics textbook

If TSH behaved like a Gaussian variable, we could measure it in a large group of healthy people and call the mean plus or minus two standard deviations “normal.” But TSH is right-skewed — closer to log-normal — and the skew persists even after careful screening.

The cleanest demonstration comes from the Hanford Thyroid Disease Study. Hamilton and colleagues isolated 766 adults with no clinical, antibody, or ultrasound evidence of thyroid disease. Their TSH distribution was still right-skewed. The 97.5th percentile was about 4.1 mIU/L, and roughly 20% of these demonstrably healthy people had a TSH above 2.5, and 10% above 3.0.

That second finding settled an argument. There was a serious proposal in the mid-2000s to lower the upper reference limit to 2.5 mIU/L. Applied to this population, it would have labeled one in five people with no detectable thyroid disease as abnormal.

Because of the skew, a TSH of 3.2 is not the mirror image of a TSH of 0.8 just because both sit a similar distance from the middle. Laboratory medicine therefore defines reference intervals by percentiles rather than standard deviations: take a carefully selected healthy population, rank the results, and call the central 95% — the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles — the reference interval.

That solves the mathematical problem. It does not solve the biological one.

Reference does not mean optimal

A 95% reference interval says exactly one thing: 95% of the selected reference population had values between these two numbers.

It does not say everyone inside the interval has optimal physiology. It does not say everyone outside it has disease. And it says nothing about where treatment starts to do more good than harm. Those are three different questions:

  1. What is statistically usual?
  2. What is physiologically normal for this person?
  3. When does intervention improve outcomes?

Medicine routinely collapses all three into one number. The third question in particular can only be answered by trials, not by distributions. When the TRUST trial randomized adults aged 65 and older with subclinical hypothyroidism (TSH 4.6–19.9 mIU/L) to levothyroxine or placebo, treatment did not improve hypothyroid symptoms or tiredness. Being outside the range and being worth treating turned out to be different things.

Every thyroid has its own normal

The hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis is a feedback loop. The pituitary senses circulating thyroid hormone and adjusts TSH, and the relationship is steeply inverse and roughly log-linear: a small change in free T4 produces a much larger change in TSH. That amplification is why TSH is such a sensitive screening test.

But people run this loop at different set points. One person lives around a TSH of 0.8; another around 2.5; another near the top of the population distribution. All three can have entirely normal thyroid physiology.

The key observation is that variation within a person is much narrower than variation between people. Andersen and colleagues sampled healthy men monthly for a year. Each man’s own range of thyroid hormone values was roughly half the width of the population range. Each person’s physiology occupies its own narrow neighborhood inside the much larger neighborhood occupied by everyone.

Laboratory medicine has precise vocabulary for this, and it deserves to be better known among clinicians:

  • Within-person biological variation (CVI). For TSH, a healthy person’s values fluctuate by roughly 20% from draw to draw.
  • Index of individuality. The ratio of within-person to between-person variation. When it is low, as it is for TSH and free T4, a population reference interval is poor at detecting a meaningful change within an individual.
  • Reference change value. How far two results must differ before the change is unlikely to be noise. For TSH it is on the order of 50–60%.

Now return to my patient. A move from 2.8 to 3.6 sits within the range of ordinary fluctuation. A move from 0.9 to 3.6 is a fourfold change, several times larger than biological and analytical noise. Both endpoints sit comfortably inside the reference interval, and the report flags neither. Only one of them is likely to mean something.

The converse also happens. Someone whose set point is naturally high may repeatedly run a TSH just above the upper reference limit, with a normal free T4 and negative antibodies, and have no meaningful thyroid failure at all. A population range cannot tell these situations apart. A person’s own history often can.

Genetics begins to explain why

Much of the variation in thyroid set point is inherited. Twin studies have long suggested this, and genome-wide association studies now map it: recent analyses have identified well over a hundred — and in the largest meta-analyses, more than 250 — independent genetic signals associated with TSH.

That suggests a different question. Instead of “Is your TSH normal compared with everyone else?” we might eventually ask “Is your TSH where we would expect it to be, given your biology?”

Kuś and colleagues took a first step in 2024. They built a polygenic score from 59 TSH-associated variants and used it to construct genetically informed TSH reference ranges across three independent European cohorts. The score explained 9–11% of the variation in TSH — several times more than free T4 (2.4–2.7%) or any single non-genetic factor they examined (0.2–1.8%).

More importantly, it changed diagnoses. Among people classified as having subclinical hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism by conventional ranges, roughly 25–30% were reclassified as euthyroid by their genetically informed range. A much smaller fraction of people with “normal” results moved in the other direction.

This is not a reason to order thyroid polygenic scores. The authors are explicit that their score captures only about 10% of between-person variation and needs refinement and validation. The cohorts were of European ancestry, and polygenic scores are known to transfer poorly across populations.

But conceptually it matters. Some of what we currently call an abnormal TSH is probably normal variation in an individual’s set point. And some values well inside the reference interval probably represent a real departure from that person’s own physiology.

Age, sex, and everything else

Genetics is not the only modifier. In NHANES III, the 97.5th percentile of TSH was about 3.6 mIU/L in people in their twenties and about 7.5 mIU/L in those over 80, and the whole distribution shifted upward with age, not just its tail. Sex, pregnancy (the one setting where we already use routinely personalized, trimester-specific ranges), iodine intake, ancestry, medications, recent illness, and time of day all move TSH. So does the assay itself, and so can biotin supplements, which interfere with many immunoassays.

So which reference population should define normal for an 85-year-old woman? All adults? Other 85-year-olds? Other women? People with similar iodine intake, or similar genetics? Or, ideally, her own measurements from when she was well?

Once you ask the question carefully, the line between normal and abnormal becomes much fuzzier than the report implies.

A caution about “optimal” ranges

If population ranges are imperfect, the tempting fix is a narrower “optimal” range. A TSH of 0.5–2.0 is a favorite in parts of wellness medicine.

That repeats the original mistake with a smaller box. It is still one range applied to everyone, and it has given up the population data that at least anchored the old one. By the Hanford data, it would label a fifth or more of healthy people as suboptimal, and it invites treatment that trials have not shown to help.

The argument of this essay is not that the reference range is too wide. It is that a range of any width answers a population question, and the patient is asking an individual one.

There are several kinds of normal

It helps to stop talking about one normal range and to name the different questions:

  • Population normal: where do 95% of appropriately selected healthy people fall?
  • Demographic normal: what is expected for someone of this age, sex, and relevant background?
  • Genetic normal: what value would we predict from this person’s inherited physiology?
  • Personal normal: where has this person’s physiology run when they were well?
  • Risk-associated threshold: at what values does future disease risk begin to change?
  • Treatment threshold: at what point does intervention actually improve outcomes?

These boundaries need not coincide, and usually don’t.

The principle extends well beyond the thyroid. Blood pressure, glucose, LDL, bone density, testosterone, kidney function and body composition all sit on continuous distributions. Medicine draws lines across them because clinicians have to make decisions. Those lines are often enormously useful. But they are what they are: decision tools laid over continuous biology, not features of the biology itself.

From reference ranges to reference models

For most of modern medicine, personalization meant adjusting a reference interval for one or two variables, such as age or sex. Computation allows something richer.

Imagine a health record that doesn’t simply print TSH 3.7 — NORMAL, but knows that:

  • your TSH averaged 1.1–1.5 over the previous decade;
  • the current value is 3.7 on two separate draws, well beyond the reference change value;
  • your free T4 has drifted down relative to your own baseline;
  • you have developed thyroid peroxidase antibodies;
  • your age, medications, and recent illnesses are accounted for;
  • and, eventually, your genetic profile predicts a relatively low set point.

The clinically relevant information is no longer whether 3.7 falls inside a population box. It is the trajectory and the context. The reference range becomes one piece of evidence inside a personal reference model.

That is where laboratory medicine is heading: away from asking whether a number is normal for a population and toward asking whether the pattern is normal for the person in front of us.

What this means in practice now

Most of that model doesn’t exist yet in a usable form. Much of the reasoning is available today:

  • Find the old results. A prior TSH from a well year is often more informative than the reference interval.
  • Judge change against biological variation, not against the range. A shift larger than roughly 50–60% deserves attention even if both values are “normal.”
  • Repeat before labeling. Ideally in the same lab, at a similar time of day, away from acute illness, with biotin stopped for a few days beforehand.
  • Add free T4 and TPO antibodies when TSH is mildly abnormal or has moved meaningfully. Direction and autoimmunity change the meaning of the number.
  • Separate diagnosis from treatment. Whether a value is outside a range and whether treatment helps are different questions, and the second is answered by trials.

For my patient, that means a repeat TSH with free T4 and antibodies, compared against her own 2017 baseline — and an honest conversation about menopause as the other thread running through her fatigue and her LDL. Not a verdict on 3.6.

Normal compared with whom?

The reference range isn’t wrong. It answers a narrower question than most people realize. Population intervals have served medicine well precisely because we lacked the information to characterize each person’s physiology individually.

Longitudinal lab data, electronic records, genetics, and better statistical models are starting to change that. The future will probably not eliminate reference ranges. It will put them in their proper place.

Because the most important question in laboratory medicine isn’t “Is this value normal?”

It is: “Normal compared with whom?”
