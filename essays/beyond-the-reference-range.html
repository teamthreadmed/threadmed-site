Beyond the Reference Range

From population thresholds to individual diagnosis in male hypogonadism.

Gonadal axisBoneMarrowAdiposeFertilityPopulation health
Anthony Pick, MD, CDCES, CCD, MSCP

Medicine cuts the body into departments. Patients arrive with threads.

Thread Medicine is what I call the work of following those threads wherever they lead — across organ systems, across the specialties that were never built to talk to each other, and across the boundary between the individual in front of you and the population he belongs to. It is applied systems biology held to an evidence standard, delivered by a team, and fitted to a person.

Testosterone is one of the better threads to pull on, because it runs through nearly everything we have carved apart: bone, marrow, adipose tissue, muscle, vasculature, mood, fertility. Pull it in one place and it moves in four others. It also runs in both directions on that last axis — the same number that decides one man’s treatment is about to be drawn on two million people at once. Which is exactly why a single value, taken once, on an unknown assay, read against a population range, is such an inadequate way to decide whether a man has enough of it.

A 52-year-old man sits across from me with a lab printout he has already read six times. Total testosterone: 400 ng/dL. Eight years ago, in a physical he barely remembers, it was 800. He is tired in a way sleep doesn’t fix, his libido has quietly left the building, and he has gained fourteen pounds around the middle that no amount of Peloton is touching.

His primary care physician told him his testosterone is normal. His primary care physician is correct. His primary care physician may also be missing the point.

This essay is about the space between those two sentences.

Why this is complicated — the short version

This essay is long because the subject is, and I’d rather show the joints than hide them. If you read nothing else, read this. Ten reasons a testosterone level is harder to interpret than almost any other common lab:

  1. A normal level can be deficient for a given man. Reference ranges describe populations; individuals have set points. Some of that variance is genetic — men with longer androgen receptor CAG repeats hold 10–14% higher free testosterone to achieve the same signal.
  2. A low total can be a normal man, and a normal total can be a low man. Total testosterone is mostly a statement about SHBG. Obesity lowers SHBG and drags the total down with free testosterone intact; aging and liver disease raise it and hide a real deficiency behind a normal number.
  3. The assay changes the answer. Reported lower limits span roughly 237–349 ng/dL across platforms. In obese men, direct immunoassay doubled the apparent rate of hypogonadism versus mass spectrometry.
  4. Circadian and pre-analytic effects are large. Time of day, fasting state, sleep, and acute illness all move the number. About a third of men whose first value is low have a normal second value.
  5. Different tissues fail at different levels. Fat accrual starts around 300–350 ng/dL; libido typically holds until under 200; erectile function until under 100. There is no single level at which a man becomes hypogonadal.
  6. The thresholds are population artifacts. 264 ng/dL is the 2.5th percentile of healthy young men — the same kind of statistical line as a T-score of −2.5, and no more physiologic.
  7. Symptoms are nonspecific. Fatigue, low libido, and mood change at 400 ng/dL have a differential that includes sleep apnea, depression, iron deficiency, alcohol, medication, and deconditioning — most of them more likely than androgen deficiency.
  8. Much of testosterone’s effect isn’t testosterone. Estradiol, made from it, is the dominant sex steroid for the male skeleton and the main regulator of fat accumulation. Suppress it and you harm the patient while the testosterone level looks fine.
  9. Key recommendations are expert opinion at low evidence grades. The AUA’s treatment target of 450–600 ng/dL is a conditional, Grade C recommendation. No randomized trial has compared target ranges for clinical outcomes.
  10. What we actually want to measure, we can’t. Androgen action happens inside cells, governed by local enzyme activity and receptor density. Serum testosterone is a proxy, and every available refinement — free testosterone, DHT, receptor genotype — is a proxy for the proxy.

None of this means the diagnosis is unknowable. It means the number is a starting point, and the reasoning that follows it is the actual clinical work.

The stakes just went up

In July 2026, the Department of Defense made this an unusually consequential question. Secretary Hegseth announced that testosterone screening would be added to the annual periodic health assessment for service members aged 30 and older, with voluntary testing below 30 and optional testosterone replacement therapy for those found deficient. The framing was readiness; the video was captioned “The High-T Department of War.”

Set the politics aside — the clinical problem is worth examining on its own terms, because it is the largest natural experiment in applying a population threshold to individuals that this field has ever run.

The professional response was immediate and, from a screening-methodology standpoint, well founded. The Endocrine Society stated that there is insufficient evidence to support population-level screening for hypogonadism in asymptomatic men, noting that testosterone measurements vary with time of day, laboratory method, sleep, and illness — and that this creates risk of both overdiagnosis and missed diagnosis. The American College of Physicians takes a similar position. Roughly a third of men whose initial testosterone tests low will have a normal value on a second draw.

Both failure modes deserve naming, because they are the same failure modes that occur one patient at a time in every clinic:

Overdiagnosis. A single draw in a population living with chronic sleep restriction, energy deficit, high training loads, and shift work will flag a great many men whose axes are transiently suppressed rather than diseased. Treat those men and you convert a reversible state into a permanent dependency — because exogenous testosterone suppresses the axis you just measured. In a force whose median age sits in the late twenties, the fertility implications are not a footnote: testosterone reduces spermatogenesis and testicular volume, and both the AUA and the Endocrine Society counsel against it in men who intend to father children.

Underdiagnosis. Less discussed, and in some ways more interesting. A screening program built on total testosterone alone will miss the man with high SHBG whose total looks reassuring while his free testosterone is frankly low. It will miss organic disease if a low value is not followed by LH, FSH, and prolactin — and this is a population with a specific, genuinely under-recognized cause of central hypogonadism: traumatic brain injury and blast exposure produce pituitary dysfunction, and that diagnosis is missed precisely when a low testosterone is treated as an isolated finding rather than as a thread leading somewhere. It will also mislabel the man whose axis is suppressed by anabolic steroid use — a documented issue in some units — as having primary disease, and treat him into exactly what he was already doing.

What has not been published — as of this writing, in August 2026 — is the part that determines whether the program helps or harms: the assay standard, the draw timing, the confirmatory protocol, the reference range, the treatment threshold, and the fertility counseling pathway. Those aren’t administrative details. They are the screening program. Wilson and Jungner would recognize the omission.

I want to be precise about what I’m criticizing, because this is a live policy and the details will change. The objection is not to screening service members. A well-specified program — LC-MS/MS or a certified assay, two morning fasting samples, SHBG and LH on any low value, a documented fertility conversation before any prescription, explicit protection against career consequences, and a stated threshold — could plausibly do more good than the status quo, particularly for the men whose low testosterone points to a pituitary injury nobody has looked for. If the Department publishes that protocol, much of what follows here becomes an argument in its favor rather than against it.

What does not change with the implementation details is the underlying problem, and it is the reason this essay exists: applying a population threshold to an individual generates error in both directions, and doing it at scale generates error at scale. Whether the program becomes good medicine depends entirely on what happens after the number comes back — which is a clinical question, not a policy one.

Which brings us to the underlying problem, one that predates any memorandum.

Two different questions

Every guideline in this field answers the same question: does this man belong to the population we call hypogonadal?

That is not the question in the room. The question in the room is: is this man androgen-insufficient relative to his own physiology, and will restoring androgen exposure change anything he cares about?

Those questions have different answers surprisingly often. The gap between them is not a failure of the guidelines — it is a category difference. A population threshold is a decision rule optimized across thousands of men to balance overdiagnosis against underdiagnosis. It is the right tool when you know nothing about the individual. It becomes a worse tool the more you know.

What follows is an attempt to spell out, nuance by nuance, what “knowing more about the individual” actually consists of — and then to assemble those nuances into something you can use on a Tuesday afternoon.

Nuance 1 — The threshold is a statistic, not a physiology

The number 264 ng/dL has acquired an authority it was never designed to carry. It is the 2.5th percentile of total testosterone in healthy, non-obese men aged 19–39, measured on a CDC-harmonized assay. That is all it is: a statistical boundary describing where the bottom 2.5% of young healthy men sit — not a biological cliff at which androgen-dependent tissues fail.

We already accept this logic elsewhere. A T-score of −2.5 does not mean bone is intact at −2.4 and shattered at −2.6; it means we drew a line on a distribution because clinical decisions require lines. Every endocrinologist knows this about bone density. Fewer of us apply the same skepticism to testosterone, where the line has hardened into something close to doctrine.

The guidelines are not identical, and the differences are instructive:

Source Diagnostic threshold
Endocrine Society <264 ng/dL, two fasting morning samples
AUA <300 ng/dL, two separate early-morning samples
Society for Endocrinology / ACB <230 ng/dL likely diagnostic; >346 ng/dL generally excludes; 230–346 is an explicit gray zone

That third framework is the only one that states the clinical truth out loud: there is a range in which the number does not decide anything. Values between roughly 230 and 346 ng/dL are seen in eugonadal and hypogonadal men alike and require clinical correlation — which is a polite way of saying they require a clinician.

The European Male Ageing Study made the same case from the symptom side. Only the sexual symptom cluster showed a syndromic association with total testosterone, and it did so below roughly 320 ng/dL. Different symptom domains, different thresholds, no single number.

Nuance 2 — Different tissues fail at different levels

The strongest evidence that one threshold is biologically implausible comes from Finkelstein’s ablate-and-add-back design: men rendered hypogonadal with a GnRH agonist, then given graded testosterone doses with or without an aromatase inhibitor. The result was not a threshold. It was a staircase.

Androgen-dependent outcome Approximate level at which it begins to fail
Fat accumulation ~300–350 ng/dL
Bone resorption <300–500 ng/dL
Lean mass and strength ≤200 ng/dL
Sexual desire ~<200 ng/dL
Erectile function <100 ng/dL

Read that table against the man at 400. Fat accrual and bone resorption are already plausibly in play at his level. Libido, on population data, should not be. And yet libido is his chief complaint.

This is the point at which diagnosis has to become multidimensional rather than binary. The staircase is not just an argument against thresholds — it is a coherence test. When a man’s symptom domain matches the level he has, the story hangs together. When it doesn’t, that discordance is information, not noise.

Nuance 3 — Symptoms are nonspecific, and the guidelines are right to be cautious

Before the individualizing argument runs away with itself, it deserves its strongest counterweight.

At 400 ng/dL, the differential for fatigue, low libido, and mood change is enormous: depression, obstructive sleep apnea, iron deficiency, thyroid disease, alcohol, insufficient sleep, deconditioning, medication effects, and aging itself all produce an identical complaint set. Guidelines deliberately err toward avoiding overdiagnosis in late-onset and functional hypogonadism precisely because the symptoms carry so little discriminating power at moderately low levels.

“He used to be 800” is also the exact argument the direct-to-consumer testosterone industry uses to medicalize normal aging in men who would be better served by treating their sleep apnea, their alcohol intake, their body composition, and their untreated depression. Any framework for individualization that cannot say no is not a framework — it is a sales funnel.

So the individualizing case has to be made carefully, and it has to be made within a workup that takes the alternatives seriously.

Nuance 4 — The individual set point is real, and partly genetic

Guidelines do not — and currently cannot — support diagnosing hypogonadism on the basis of relative decline. There is no validated evidence that a 50% drop within the reference range constitutes disease.

And yet the AUA’s 2024 guideline contains a genuinely interesting concession: where reliable prior off-therapy testosterone data exist, clinicians may consider titrating therapy to return the patient toward his own documented baseline. That is expert opinion, not trial evidence. But it is a formal acknowledgment inside a major guideline that an individual set point is a real construct and the population range is a compromise.

There is also mechanism behind it. The androgen receptor gene carries a polymorphic CAG repeat encoding a polyglutamine tract in its transactivation domain; longer repeats reduce receptor transcriptional activity. Across two independent cohorts (n = 2,322 and 358), men with longer CAG repeats carried 10–14% higher circulating free testosterone — the axis holding a higher level to compensate for a less responsive receptor. Individual set points are not a rhetorical device. Some of that variance is genotype.

The honest position is narrow and worth stating precisely:

A documented prior baseline is a legitimate target once you have established a diagnosis. It is not, by itself, a legitimate basis for the diagnosis.

Hold that line and you can practice individualized medicine without becoming a testosterone dispensary.

Nuance 5 — The number may not be real

We interpret testosterone values as though the number in the chart were the number in the man. Often it isn’t, and the errors come in two layers.

Pre-analytic. Fasting, before 10 AM, on two separate mornings, not during acute illness. Glucose intake acutely lowers testosterone; diurnal amplitude is substantial in younger men; transient central suppression during illness is the rule rather than the exception. A single non-fasting afternoon draw during a viral illness is not a datum, it is a rumor.

Analytic. This is the layer most clinicians never examine:

  • CDC-certified immunoassays are now genuinely good — slopes of 0.98–1.06 against LC-MS/MS, average bias within ±6.4%. Non-certified platforms are not; one evaluation found a widely used analyzer biased roughly 20% low.
  • The bias is not evenly distributed. In young obese non-diabetic men, direct immunoassay read lower than mass spectrometry and doubled the apparent rate of hypogonadism — 53.7% versus 26.3%. The population most likely to be mislabeled is precisely the population most likely to be tested.
  • Reported lower limits of normal range from about 8.2 to 12.1 nmol/L (roughly 237–349 ng/dL) across platforms. A man can change diagnosis by changing health systems. That spread is essentially the entire gray zone.
  • Below about 100 ng/dL, immunoassay performance degrades badly — correlations as low as r ≈ 0.40 on some platforms.

Practically: know which method your lab runs, use the same platform for serial comparison, and reach for LC-MS/MS when the value is low, the patient is obese, or the decision is close.

Nuance 6 — Total testosterone is a statement about binding proteins

Total testosterone measures hormone that is overwhelmingly protein-bound. What the androgen receptor sees is the free and loosely albumin-bound fraction. Whenever sex hormone–binding globulin is perturbed — and in cardiometabolic practice it is perturbed constantly — total testosterone becomes a distorted lens.

SHBG Direction of error Typical setting
Low (<20 nmol/L) Total T reads falsely low Obesity, insulin resistance, T2D, glucocorticoids, hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome
High (>50 nmol/L) Total T reads falsely normal Aging, hepatic disease, hyperthyroidism, anticonvulsants, HIV, low energy availability
Normal Free T adds nothing Don’t order it reflexively

The low-SHBG pattern is the single most common source of overdiagnosis in men referred for “low T.” A 46-year-old with a BMI of 36, an SHBG of 14, and a total testosterone of 250 very often has a normal free testosterone and does not have an androgen deficiency problem — he has a metabolic problem wearing an endocrine costume.

When SHBG is abnormal, get a real free testosterone:

  • Direct analog immunoassays are prohibited by every major guideline and should never inform a decision. They measure a fraction of true free T that itself varies with SHBG.
  • Equilibrium dialysis is the reference method, though even ED has thinner outcome-correlation data than its status implies.
  • Calculated free testosterone (Vermeulen) is the right practical default. It overestimates dialysis by about 19% (median ratio 1.19), but that bias is largely independent of SHBG, albumin, and testosterone concentration — which is exactly what you want in a formula. The Ly and Zakharov equations carry more SHBG-dependent error.

Two traps. First, every calculation inherits the accuracy of the total testosterone and SHBG assays feeding it. Second, watch the units: 5–9 ng/dL and 50–90 pg/mL are the same range, and I have seen that conversion mangled in charts, portals, and patient-facing summaries more than once.

Nuance 7 — Localize before you label

LH and FSH belong on every confirmed low value, because they determine whether you are looking at a disease or a state.

  • High LH/FSH → primary (testicular). Examine the testes and estimate volume; consider karyotype when volume is small and FSH high. This is organic, permanent, and treated indefinitely.
  • Low or inappropriately normal LH/FSH → secondary (central). Check prolactin, ferritin and transferrin saturation, and the remaining anterior pituitary axes. Image the pituitary when testosterone is markedly low (conventionally <150 ng/dL), prolactin is elevated, other axes are involved, or there are headaches or visual symptoms.
  • Normal LH with a borderline testosterone is the informative middle case. An LH sitting in the upper half of its range alongside a low-normal testosterone suggests the axis is already working to hold that level — a quiet argument that this man’s own set point is higher than where he currently sits. This is the closest thing we have to an internal control.

And the ordering rule that is not negotiable: if fertility is on the table at all, obtain a semen analysis before writing anything. Exogenous testosterone suppresses spermatogenesis. This is the most common irreversible error in the field.

Nuance 8 — Establish reversibility before establishing a diagnosis

Functional hypogonadism — reversible suppression of the axis rather than disease of it — is the most common form and the most commonly mistreated. Work the list explicitly rather than by impression:

  • Obesity and insulin resistance (the dominant driver)
  • Obstructive sleep apnea — ask, then actually test
  • Opioids — profoundly suppressive and often the entire explanation
  • Glucocorticoids, including inhaled and injected
  • Anabolic-androgenic steroids, SARMs, and “peptides” — the single most consequential history in 2026. Post-AAS hypogonadism is now common in men in their thirties and forties and presents identically to primary or secondary disease depending on where in recovery the axis sits. Ask directly, non-judgmentally, and more than once. If you don’t, you won’t be told.
  • Alcohol; heavy cannabis; energy deficit and overtraining
  • Chronic systemic illness; gabapentinoids; prolactin-raising agents

In functional disease, the driver is treated first and the axis re-measured after a meaningful interval. This is also where the work stops being a physician task. Sleep apnea adherence, meaningful weight loss, alcohol reduction, and training load are not solved in a twenty-minute visit by a prescription; they are solved by a team — coaching, sleep medicine, nutrition, sometimes behavioral health — with the physician holding the thread. A practice that cannot deliver that has only one lever available, which is a large part of why the prescription gets written. In the GLP-1 era this is no longer a stalling tactic: incretin-based weight loss raises total testosterone, LH, and FSH in proportion to weight lost, and unlike exogenous testosterone it does not suppress spermatogenesis. For a 38-year-old with obesity, a testosterone of 240, and a wife who wants a second child, the correct first-line therapy in 2026 is often not testosterone at all.

Nuance 9 — Estradiol belongs in the diagnostic frame

Estradiol is a male hormone. Circulating E2 in men exceeds that of postmenopausal women, and much of what we credit to testosterone is the work of testosterone after aromatization.

The diagnostic relevance is sharpest in bone. Mendelian randomization across 175,583 men found a causal effect of estradiol on fracture risk — an odds ratio of 1.75 for major osteoporotic fracture per standard-deviation decrease in E2 — while testosterone showed no causal effect on fracture at all. Men in the lowest quartile of bioavailable E2 in the MINOS cohort had 6.6–8.7% lower BMD across sites.

So when you are working up a man with unexplained low bone density, the sex-steroid question is not only “is his testosterone adequate” but “is he aromatizing enough.” And if he arrives on an aromatase inhibitor with a single-digit estradiol, that is not a reassuring finding — that is a diagnosis.

Measure it by LC-MS/MS when you measure it. Immunoassay estradiol is unreliable at male concentrations, for the same cross-reactivity reasons that make immunoassay DHT worthless.

Nuance 10 — What we actually want to measure, we can’t

Serum testosterone is a proxy for the thing we care about: androgen signaling inside cells. Intracellular exposure depends on local 5α-reductase activity, aldo-keto reductase metabolism, receptor density, coregulator availability, and chromatin architecture. The prostate maintains intraprostatic DHT roughly fivefold above intraprostatic testosterone, largely uncoupled from serum DHT. Two men with identical serum values can have meaningfully different tissue signaling.

There is no validated clinical assay for this, and the candidates are further away than they look:

  • DHT is not recommended for diagnosis, and the assay problem is severe — immunoassay values run from 79% to more than 1000% above LC-MS/MS. One commercial EIA was formally invalidated after failing to detect finasteride-induced suppression. If you order DHT, order LC-MS/MS or don’t order it. (One provocative exception worth holding, not acting on: in biochemically eugonadal men with hypogonadal symptoms, serum DHT independently predicted symptom severity on the AMS scale.)
  • AR CAG genotyping is commercially available and explains real variance in set point, but in 181,217 UK Biobank men the effects were small, did not extend to most androgen-related outcomes, and — critically — adjusting for repeat length did not change the relationship between testosterone and clinical outcomes. Don’t order it.
  • Proteomic markers of androgen bioactivity (4HPPD, IGFBP-6) and in vitro AR-reporter bioassays are the most interesting tools in this space and remain strictly research instruments.

What we can do is triangulate with end-organ surrogates. This is where practicing endocrinologists add value that a portal value never will:

Surrogate What it tells you
Hemoglobin/hematocrit trend over years Erythropoiesis is androgen-responsive; unexplained downward drift is real signal
LH position relative to current T Whether the axis agrees with the reference range
BMD (and TBS where available) Integrates sex-steroid exposure over years — remember E2 dominates here
Body composition (serial lean and fat mass) Fat accrual is the most T-sensitive change; lean mass loss implies deeper deficiency
Testicular volume Structural evidence the axis has been down a long time
IIEF, AMS scored serially Converts “he feels better” into something measurable

Assembling it: a staged approach

Each stage narrows the question. The movement is always the same: population reference → the individual’s own reference → evidence of end-organ effect.

0. Should you test at all? Sexual symptom cluster, osteoporosis or low-trauma fracture, unexplained normocytic anemia, small or shrinking testes, gynecomastia, infertility, or a high-prior-probability condition. Not a wellness panel. Not isolated fatigue with no other domain involved. Not during acute illness.

1. Is the number real? Two fasting morning samples, known assay, consistent platform, LC-MS/MS when the decision is close.

2. Correct for binding. SHBG with every diagnostic testosterone; calculated or dialysis free T when SHBG is abnormal. Reason downstream from the binding-corrected estimate, not the total.

3. Localize. LH/FSH always; prolactin, iron studies, pituitary imaging and karyotype by indication; semen analysis before anything if fertility matters.

4. Establish reversibility. Work the functional list explicitly. Treat the driver, then re-measure.

5. Build the individual reference. Historical off-therapy values, axis position, end-organ surrogates, and symptom-threshold coherence.

6. Read the coherence. Three patterns, three actions:

  • Concordant deficient — binding-corrected androgen clearly low, symptom domain matches the level, surrogates support it. Diagnostic confidence is high; treat once reversible drivers are addressed.
  • Concordant sufficient — androgen adequate, symptom domain implausible at that level, surrogates unremarkable. Do not treat. Go find the real diagnosis. This is not a failed visit; it is the visit.
  • Discordant — the man at 400. This is where judgment is required. At population scale, this is also the largest of the three groups, which is why mass screening generates so much work and so little clarity.

7. For the discordant man, design the trial rather than drifting into it. Before the first dose, write down the primary endpoint and the instrument, the objective markers, the target, the duration (3–6 months at target), and — most importantly — the stopping rule. If the level is at target and the endpoint has not moved, therapy stops; the symptoms were not androgen-dependent. Say this to the patient before starting, because it is far harder to say afterward.

The single most useful habit in the whole framework is to name the pattern in the note. “Discordant: TT 400 ×2 by LC-MS/MS, SHBG normal, cFT low-normal, LH 7.8, documented TT 800 at age 44, Hgb fallen from 15.6 to 14.1 over six years, AMS 42, OSA treated, no AAS exposure.” That is a defensible clinical position. “Low T” is not.

The other mass screening program

While the Pentagon was drafting a memorandum, the market had already built its own version — larger, faster, and answerable to no one.

Let me be fair before I’m critical, because the criticism only lands if the fair part is true. Direct-to-consumer men’s health platforms grew for real reasons. Men underuse primary care. Symptoms like fatigue and low libido are frequently dismissed in short visits. Getting a morning fasting testosterone, an SHBG, an LH, and a follow-up appointment through conventional channels can take two months and three phone calls, and plenty of men have been told “your labs are normal” without anyone examining what normal meant for them. That gap is genuine, and it is partly our fault. Some of these platforms have brought real attention to men who had none.

Now the evidence. In a secret-shopper study of seven DTC testosterone platforms published in JAMA Internal Medicine, the fictitious patient was a 34-year-old with low energy, low libido, normal total and free testosterone, and a stated desire for future fertility. Six of the seven — 85.7% — offered him testosterone therapy anyway. Only one of the seven asked about recent cardiovascular events or about his interest in having children.

Read that against everything above. Normal free testosterone. Fertility explicitly on the table. Six offers of a drug that suppresses spermatogenesis.

The structural problem is not villainy, and framing it that way lets it off the hook too easily. It is that a business whose revenue depends on continued prescriptions cannot credibly operate a stopping rule. Every safeguard in this essay — treat the reversible driver first, conclude sufficient when the evidence says so, discontinue at 3–6 months if the endpoint hasn’t moved — costs the platform its customer. A model can be staffed entirely by well-meaning clinicians and still, in aggregate, be unable to say no. That is what a misaligned incentive looks like from the inside: not corruption, but a set of questions that never quite get asked.

Social media compounds it in a specific, physiologic way. Recall the asymmetry: the dose-response for lean mass is continuous with no plateau, while libido, mood, and cognition stop responding well within the physiologic range. A man raising his dose therefore gets visible, photographable reinforcement — the one domain that keeps rewarding him — while the domains that motivated him plateau and the risks accumulate silently in his hematocrit and his sperm count. Testimonial content selects for responders and has no denominator: the men who felt nothing, stopped, or became infertile do not post. Physiology and the content economy point the same direction, which is why “optimization” is a very difficult argument to counter with a reference range.

The honest response is not to defend the guidelines harder. It is to close the gap that created the market — to actually work up the symptomatic man in the gray zone rather than dismissing him, to give him the reasoning rather than a number, and to be willing to treat him when the evidence supports it and equally willing to stop. A clinic that can say both is doing something no platform structurally can.

I build clinical decision support, so I’ll say the uncomfortable part directly: everything above is exactly the kind of reasoning that gets turned into an algorithm, and most of the ways that goes wrong are ways it goes wrong quietly.

Thresholds are trivially easy to encode. Judgment is not. A rule that fires “low testosterone” at 299 and stays silent at 301 will be built in an afternoon; the staged reasoning that decides whether either number means anything takes a clinician. The risk of automating this field is not that machines will reason badly — it is that they will encode the population-over-individual error and then execute it perfectly, a few million times.

Four cautions I would apply to any tool in this space, mine included:

Interrogate what the training data encode. Reference ranges here derive from cohorts of predominantly healthy, non-obese, young men, and much of the real-world testosterone data on which a model would train was generated on immunoassays that read systematically low in obese men. A model learns those artifacts as if they were physiology. It will then be most confident about the patients it understands least.

Do not build demographic corrections into the math. Medicine has run this experiment already with race-corrected eGFR and spirometry, and it went badly in a specific, instructive way: a population-level association was hard-coded as a per-patient adjustment, and it systematically changed who qualified for care. Ancestry is not a mechanism. Adiposity, SHBG, assay method, sleep, and medication are mechanisms — model those. If a variable’s only justification is that it improved fit, it does not belong in a clinical rule.

Watch what the tool assumes about resources. An algorithm that recommends LC-MS/MS, equilibrium dialysis, polysomnography, DXA, and a coaching program is describing my practice, not most practices. Deployed unchanged into a setting that funds none of those, it doesn’t produce better care — it produces a documented gap between the recommended workup and the delivered one, and the patients on the wrong side of that gap will not be randomly distributed. Personalized medicine that only personalizes for the well-resourced is population medicine with extra steps.

Model current practice and you inherit current practice. A system trained on prescribing data from a market that overtreats will learn to overtreat, fluently and with citations. The corrective is to hold the tool to the evidence rather than to the observed behavior — and to make it show which stage of the reasoning drove its output, so a clinician can disagree with a specific step rather than accept or reject a verdict.

The useful role for software here is not to answer the question. It is to make sure the question was asked properly: was this a morning fasting sample, is there a second one, do we have SHBG, has an LH been drawn, was the medication and substance history taken, is there a prior off-therapy value buried in a chart from 2018. That is real work, it is work clinicians reliably skip under time pressure, and no judgment is displaced by doing it.

What this framework does not do

Honesty about limits is part of the method.

  • No composite of this kind is validated against outcomes. The components are evidence-based; their combination is reasoned, not tested.
  • Relative decline remains unvalidated as a diagnostic criterion. Everything above is designed to keep “target” and “basis” separate.
  • Tissue-level androgen action remains unmeasurable. Every surrogate here is indirect.
  • It can be misused. Any structure that legitimizes treating men inside the reference range can be turned into a rationale for treating everyone. The safeguards are Stage 4 (reversibility first), the discipline to conclude sufficient at Stage 6, and Stage 7’s stopping rule. Remove those three and this becomes precisely the thing it was written against.

The man at 400

The wrong answer is “your testosterone is normal.” The other wrong answer is “you’ve dropped 50%, let’s start testosterone.”

The right answer is a workup that treats the number as one variable in a system: repeat morning fasting testosterone on a known assay with SHBG and LH, calculated or dialysis free testosterone, prolactin and iron studies, a genuine sleep apnea assessment, a medication and substance history that includes AAS and opioids, a depression screen, the hemoglobin trend over a decade, body composition, and — if the picture stays ambiguous — a DXA. Then treat what is reversible, and reassess.

If, after all that, he is androgen-insufficient on the composite picture, treat him and measure whether it worked. If he isn’t, you have still found the thing that is actually wrong with him, which is the entire job.

Use the population threshold to identify who is clearly deficient. Use the individual’s own history, axis, and end-organ evidence to decide what to do about everyone else — and pre-commit to the endpoint that will tell you when you were wrong.

That discipline is what a screening program cannot supply and a clinician can. Two million annual testosterone levels will not produce two million diagnoses; they will produce two million starting points, most of which lead somewhere other than the gonads. Whether that becomes good medicine or a very expensive sorting error depends entirely on what happens after the number comes back.

The threshold was never the diagnosis. It was only ever the place we agreed to stop arguing.

Thread Medicine — applied systems biology, held to an evidence standard, delivered by a team, and fitted to a person. Following the threads that run between organ systems, because patients don’t arrive in departments.
